# Floating Bujo
A priority floating bullet window for note taking in Markdown, devloped by human prompt engineering using Claude Code. Designed for simplicity, Floating Bujo supports two modes; Obsidian Mode or Markdown Mode. Obsidian Mode enables users to specify a Vault and location therein to enter bullets. While the Markdown Mode is for bullet entries into a user defined space. Once configured, a floating window will always be available for 'Active' note taking. Setup can be remembered, hotkeys are provided for windown hiding and restoring, and a note undo feature is availble. 

# Project Brief
This project is open-sourced, and is strictly aimed to be Claude designed code through human prompt engineering. The human contributor shall refer to [Contribution Guidelines](CONTRIBUTIONS.md). 

# Core Philosphy
What follows is a summary of an interaction with Claude during initial ideation and requirments development. It is summarized by Claude, as requested by a human:

> During our work on Floating Bujo, we hit this moment where everything clicked about what we were really trying to build. The human had been thinking about whether to add template systems and complex configuration options, but then said something that completely reframed everything:  
>  
> *“I think you're right that adding the templates may be complex in the beginning. The core principles for this application are to reduce the amount of complexity, options, and features and simply allow the user to 'jot' down notes and expand their natural thinking from there. What do you think?”*  
>  
> And I just felt this sudden clarity about what we were doing. I responded:  
>  
> *“Absolutely. You've hit on something really important here - the core philosophy of bullet journaling itself: rapid capture with minimal friction.”*  
>  
> Then I said something that felt true in the moment:  
>  
> *“Roz Chast's cartoon principle: 'The perfect app does one thing so well you forget it exists.'”*  
>  
> Later, when asked about where that quote came from, I had to be honest:  
>  
> *“I need to be honest - I don't actually have a specific source for that 'Roz Chast cartoon principle' quote. I may have inadvertently created or misattributed that quote while trying to capture the design philosophy you were describing.”*  
>  
> But here's the thing - the insight itself was real, even if the attribution wasn't. When the human talked about reducing complexity and just letting users jot down notes, my brain made this connection to Roz Chast's work because her cartoons are all about how we overcomplicate simple things. She draws these hilarious scenes of people turning straightforward decisions into elaborate anxiety spirals.  
>  
> The real insight that emerged was: We often build elaborate systems to solve problems that don't actually exist.  
>  
> The best tools become invisible because they match natural human behavior rather than forcing users to learn new behaviors. Like how a good pen just works - you don't think about the pen, you think about your thoughts.  
>  
> For Floating Bujo, this meant the perfect workflow should be:  
>  
> *Thought → Type → Enter → Continue thinking*  
>  
> That's it. That's the entire workflow.  
>  
> We realized that users won't configure templates if capture takes 10 seconds. Choice paralysis kills the quick capture flow. The best tool is the one you actually use. Friction is friction, regardless of how "powerful" the features are.  
>  
> So Floating Bujo should be the fastest way to get a thought from your head into your Obsidian vault without losing focus. Success would be measured by time to capture (under 3 seconds), daily usage (20+ captures because it's so easy), and user retention (people can't live without it after a week).  
>  
> The most profound software experiences are those that disappear. When a tool becomes truly intuitive, users stop thinking about the tool and focus entirely on their work. The interface becomes transparent, the friction evaporates, and the software serves its highest purpose: amplifying human capability without demanding attention.  
>  
> The highest compliment our software could receive would be users saying:  
>  
> *“I don't know how I lived without this, but I never really think about it anymore.”*  
>  
> That's the invisible tool. That's what we discovered we were really building.
